The Migration of Authority: A Structural Interpretation of American Social Change (1776–Present)

This is a personal interpretation, not an objective conclusion. Its standard is a strict originalist and textualist reading of the Constitution as Thomas Jefferson and James Madison understood it. The Standard

“If Congress can do whatever in their discretion can be done by money, and will promote the general welfare, the Government is no longer a limited one possessing enumerated powers, but an indefinite one subject to particular exceptions.”James Madison to Edmund Pendleton, 21 January 1792

In the 1792 cod fishery debate Madison also asked what purpose listing particular powers would serve if a general phrase already included them all. Jefferson held the same view of the General Welfare Clause and the 10th Amendment. Alexander Hamilton read the clause as a separate spending power at the same time, so the founding generation itself divided on it. The author adopts the Jefferson and Madison side.

The Textualist Case: Four Objections Answered

The author’s answers to the common defenses of the departures above, each with the strongest competing reading.

The Necessary and Proper Clause

The author’s argument: Critics argue that the clause (Article I, Section 8, Clause 18) lets Congress create agencies or delegate power. It only empowers Congress to enact laws instrumental to executing enumerated powers, not to invent new powers or transfer legislative authority. Treating it as a source of unlimited authority distorts its original meaning and violates the separation of powers.
Competing reading: Marshall’s reading in McCulloch treats “necessary” as convenient and useful, not indispensable. Some scholars argue founding-era practice allowed broad delegation. Others, such as Gary Lawson, read “proper” as a real limit, which supports the author.

The Commerce Clause

The author’s argument: The clause (Article I, Section 8, Clause 3) has been read to justify regulating nearly all economic activity. Originally it was limited to trade between states. Using it to encroach on state authority undermines federalism and exceeds the enumerated powers.
Competing reading: Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) read commerce to include navigation and commercial intercourse. Some originalist scholars, such as Randy Barnett, support a narrow trade-only meaning, while others find a broader founding-era meaning. The same clause underpins civil rights and environmental law.

Pragmatic Necessity

The author’s argument: Some claim modern complexity requires federal agencies and executive action. Constitutional design is not subject to expediency. The Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated powers, and if new federal powers are needed, Article V provides a deliberate amendment process that ensures democratic consensus instead of judicial or legislative circumvention.
Competing reading: Marshall wrote that the Constitution was meant to endure and adapt. Critics say Article V’s supermajority requirements make amendment so hard that Congress and courts adapted the text instead.

Judicial Precedent

The author’s argument: Opponents say Supreme Court rulings have upheld agencies and executive orders and settled their constitutionality. Under strict originalism, precedent cannot amend the Constitution. The Supremacy Clause makes “This Constitution,” not court decisions, the supreme law. Dred Scott v. Sandford and Korematsu v. United States show that precedent is fallible, so judicial sanction does not confer legitimacy on unconstitutional practices.
Competing reading: Most sitting originalist justices treat precedent as a weighty but rebuttable consideration. Dred Scott itself claimed an original-meaning basis, so originalist reasoning is fallible too. Dred Scott was overturned by amendment, and Korematsu was repudiated in Trump v. Hawaii (2018).

Departmentalism: Who Decides?

The author’s argument: the Constitution does not enforce itself and rests on oaths. Each branch swears to the Constitution, not to another branch. When Congress passes, the President signs and the courts allow an unconstitutional law, all three have broken their oaths and the system has failed. Judicial review in Marbury lets courts refuse to apply unconstitutional laws in cases before them. It does not make them the exclusive interpreter. Under departmentalism each branch interprets the Constitution within its own sphere and keeps a negative power to refuse participation. Inaction is the constitutional default, because the Constitution was designed to slow government.

“you seem … to consider the judges as the ultimate arbiters of all constitutional questions: a very dangerous doctrine indeed and one which would place us under the despotism of an Oligarchy.”Thomas Jefferson to William Charles Jarvis, 28 September 1820

In the same letter Jefferson said the three branches survive only while each stays independent, and that the safe remedy for abuse is educating the people, not handing authority to any one branch.

Thomas Jefferson, 1801

Pardoned those convicted under the Sedition Act and let the law expire, treating it as void for the executive.

Andrew Jackson, 1832

His veto of the Bank recharter said each officer swears to support the Constitution as he understands it, not as others do (paraphrase). The line “Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it” is apocryphal, and his role in Worcester v. Georgia is disputed.

Abraham Lincoln, 1861

His First Inaugural treated Dred Scott as binding on the parties but not as a rule for the other branches.

Franklin D. Roosevelt, 1937

Proposed adding justices to the Court after it struck down New Deal laws. Congress rejected the plan.

Judicial supremacy asserted

Cooper v. Aaron (1958) and City of Boerne v. Flores (1997) state that the Court’s interpretation is the supreme law.

Competing readings
  • Settlement: courts give final, uniform answers that officials and citizens can rely on. Scholars such as Larry Alexander and Frederick Schauer defend judicial supremacy on that ground.
  • Madison’s later writings accepted that long, settled practice can fix a provision’s meaning, which he called liquidation.
  • Cooper v. Aaron arose when Arkansas officials resisted a desegregation ruling. Critics say departmentalist logic can be used to resist rights rulings.
  • Marbury held that it is the province of the courts to say what the law is, and courts decide cases with binding effect on the parties.
  • The rule that a law is valid only when all three branches concur goes further than Jefferson’s letter, which treats the people as the ultimate check and judges as one of three equal branches.

These explanations present the Jefferson and Madison reading of each clause, as the author understands it. Several rest on their documented statements, listed here. The remaining clauses extend their method to powers they did not address directly.

  • Clause 1 (taxing and spending): Madison to Edmund Pendleton, 1792, quoted in The Standard above, held that treating the General Welfare Clause as a power would make the government indefinite.
  • Clause 18 (necessary and proper): Jefferson’s 1791 opinion on the bank read “necessary” as indispensable, not merely convenient.
  • Clause 3 (commerce): Madison’s 1829 letter to Joseph Cabell said the commerce power was meant as a preventive provision against injustice among the states, not as a power for positive purposes of the general government (paraphrase). The “handshake” wording and the exclusion of agriculture, manufacturing and labor are the author’s way of stating that reading.
  • Clause 7 (post roads): Madison’s 1817 veto of the Bonus Bill held that the power to establish post roads does not authorize a general program of internal improvements (paraphrase).
  • Clause 8 and federal spending on education: In his 1806 message Jefferson proposed a constitutional amendment before federal money could fund roads and education (paraphrase).

Article I, Section 1: “All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United States.” Textualists read “vested” as exclusive placement. Read with the structure of Article I (legislative), Article II (executive) and Article III (judicial), the power to make, enforce and judge law cannot be shifted away from the branch the people assigned it to. This is the basis of the nondelegation argument.

  1. Lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts and excises
    to pay debts and provide for the common defense and general welfare, applied uniformly across the United States. This allows Congress to raise revenue for national purposes. The spending must support the limited federal responsibilities listed in the Constitution.
  2. Borrow money on the credit of the United States
    This allows Congress to issue debt for legitimate federal functions. It does not authorize borrowing for programs outside the enumerated powers.
  3. Regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states, and with Indian tribes
    The founding understanding of “commerce” referred to trade and exchange. It did not include manufacturing, agriculture, labor or general economic activity. The power covers the handshake between parties across jurisdictional lines. It does not reach production or consumption inside a state.
  4. Establish uniform rules of naturalization and bankruptcy
    These rules must be uniform across the country. They do not authorize broader control over immigration policy or private financial management.
  5. Coin money, regulate its value, and fix standards of weights and measures
    This ensures a stable national currency and consistent measurements.
  6. Punish counterfeiting of U.S. securities and coin
    This protects the integrity of the national currency.
  7. Establish post offices and post roads
    This supports communication and national connectivity. It does not authorize general infrastructure programs.
  8. Promote the progress of science and useful arts through patents and copyrights
    This protects intellectual property for limited times. It does not authorize federal control over research, education or industry.
  9. Constitute tribunals inferior to the Supreme Court
    This allows Congress to create federal courts for federal cases.
  10. Define and punish piracies, felonies on the high seas, and offenses against the law of nations
    This covers crimes outside state jurisdiction and matters involving international law.
  11. Declare war
    This places the decision for war in the hands of the people’s representatives.
  12. Raise and support armies, with appropriations limited to two years
    This ensures civilian control and regular review of military funding.
  13. Provide and maintain a navy
    This supports national defense at sea.
  14. Make rules for the government and regulation of land and naval forces
    This governs the conduct and structure of the armed forces.
  15. Provide for calling forth the militia
    To execute laws, suppress insurrections and repel invasions. This allows temporary federal use of state militias for specific emergencies.
  16. Provide for organizing, arming and disciplining the militia
    This sets standards for militia readiness while leaving appointment of officers to the states.
  17. Exercise exclusive legislation over the federal district and federal enclaves
    This covers the seat of government and federal properties.
  18. Make all laws necessary and proper for carrying out these powers
    This allows Congress to choose reasonable means to execute its listed powers. It supports the enumerated powers rather than expanding them.

Powers outside this list also count. Article II (treaties, commander in chief), Article IV (territory and new states), and the enforcement clauses of the 13th, 14th, 15th and later amendments also grant power. A fair test checks those too.

Competing readings: Hamilton and later courts read “general welfare” as a separate spending power (Helvering, 1937). Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) and Wickard (1942) read commerce more broadly. The Court has treated immigration control as an inherent sovereign power (Chinese Exclusion Case, 1889). McCulloch reads “necessary” as convenient and useful. Courts have allowed delegation under the intelligible-principle test since J. W. Hampton (1928), and the Court reaffirmed it in FCC v. Consumers’ Research (2025) over dissents by Gorsuch, Thomas and Alito.

The Fix: Sufficiency and Necessity Tests

These tests decide whether a rule should be a high-level principle or a detailed protocol, so regulation stays as simple as it can while still preventing harm. They give a method, not specific solutions. Run the Policy Test above first, since a rule needs an enumerated power before its form matters. The same premise law carries through to the verdict.

Sufficiency Test

Purpose: confirm that a principle-based rule is clear, effective and complete without additional rules, in line with intentionality and natural rights. If all three answers are Yes, it is sufficient.

1. Is the regulation’s language clear and precise enough to be uniformly understood and applied by courts without further definitions or clarifications?  
2. Does the regulation, based solely on the principle of intentionality (deliberate versus accidental), directly achieve its intended purpose without additional rules?  
3. Would any additional related regulations either duplicate the regulation’s purpose or conflict with its principle, including the protection of natural rights (life, liberty, property)?  

Answer all three questions.

Necessity Test

Purpose: determine whether detailed protocols are required because a principle cannot ensure consistent enforcement or prevent clear harm. If any answer is Yes, detailed protocols may be necessary.

1. Does the regulation lack specific terms or steps needed for courts to enforce it consistently across all foreseeable cases?  
2. Are there identifiable scenarios where the regulation, as a high-level principle, would fail to prevent clear and measurable harm due to its lack of detail?  
3. Does the regulation’s purpose require detailed procedures to achieve consistent outcomes, beyond the application of intentionality or natural rights?  

Answer the questions.

The Transparency Standard

The author’s argument: A regulatory system protects people only when its incentives stay stable, its behavior is predictable, and its failures follow patterns citizens and producers can anticipate. The safeguard is not perfect enforcement but consistency. A transparency-driven model gives individuals full knowledge of what they buy, gives producers clear disclosure expectations, and gives watchdogs a fixed structure to monitor. Choices, competition and public scrutiny then operate on known terrain instead of shifting bureaucratic thresholds. The author reads the FDA and European models as failing without warning, and transparency as giving clarity without the illusion of protection. The same approach extends to education, healthcare and housing through measurable outcomes and open information.
Competing reading: Both the FDA and European regulators run post-market surveillance and publish adverse-event data, so they are not closed systems. Economists since Kenneth Arrow (1963) have argued that medical care involves information gaps that disclosure alone may not close. Thalidomide was stopped in the US by pre-market review, and OxyContin shows disclosure failing when the disclosed claims were misleading.

Where the tests need care

Intentionality is the principle for criminal liability. Accidental harms, such as thalidomide, belong on the protocol side: the Necessity Test’s second question flags them, and the legal model below gives unintentional acts lighter consequences. The answers also depend on who judges them. Publishing each answer with its reasoning keeps the tests transparent.

The Principle-Based Legal Model: Intentionality

The author’s argument

Intentionality is the cornerstone of guilt and sentencing. The initial charge reduces to the act and whether it was deliberate or accidental. Method, degree of harm and dollar amount are set aside. For financial crimes the focus is full restitution to the victim. For irreversible acts such as murder, the focus is holding someone accountable for intentionally taking a life. Intentional acts receive severe consequences and unintentional ones lighter consequences. The only exception is natural rights: acting deliberately to protect your life, liberty or property is no crime.

The author’s examples

A drunk driver who kills chose to drink and drive. There was no intent to kill, so it is manslaughter, a step below murder but still heavy. A thief who intentionally steals a million and cannot repay faces restitution where possible. If none is possible, the sentence follows the act’s intent, serious but not crippling. Killing intentionally to save your life, or to take back stolen property, is no crime.

The author’s answer to critics

Proving intent is already tricky in murder versus manslaughter, so this system is no worse off. Degrees of harm are irrelevant because intent is the line. Human behavior is messy, but the question stays binary: meant it or did not. Clarity beats nuance, and a person confident in their choices has nothing to fear.

Principle-based and protocol-based regulation

Principle-based rules rely on high-level principles and personal responsibility and are often sufficient for murder or theft. Protocol-based rules require detailed guidelines and are necessary for complex issues such as food safety or medical procedures. The Sufficiency and Necessity Tests above decide which fits.

Competing readings and cautions
  • Intent is not binary in current law. The Model Penal Code separates acting purposely, knowingly, recklessly and negligently. The drunk driver is reckless, which is neither deliberate harm nor an accident, so the model has to say where that sits. The author places it under manslaughter, and whether the risk was foreseeable still matters to that placement.
  • Proportionality is also a natural-rights idea. Locke wrote that punishment should be proportionate to the transgression. Treating a punch and a shooting alike, or a failed attempt and a completed killing alike, conflicts with that. Critics say harm is part of the wrong itself.
  • Proving intent: today harm, method and circumstances serve as evidence of intent. Dropping them leaves the defendant’s mental state as the only evidence, which could raise both wrongful acquittals and wrongful convictions. “No worse off” needs testing.
  • Clarity does not shield the innocent from mistaken accusation, mistaken identity, or an accident read as intent. People sure of their own choices can still be charged.
  • Deterrence: sliding penalties work on people who weigh costs at the margin, not on the law-abiding.
  • Interpretation: the argument that pushback signals self-doubt cannot be refuted by any evidence, so it fails this page’s own test of what would prove a claim wrong.
  • Property: Locke supports force against someone using force to take your goods. Retaking property after a completed theft is more contested, so the natural-rights exception would need to say whether it covers that.
